#ABSTRACT
#P Rental housing is one of the largest parts of the urban economy in India and one of the least organised. Paying guest establishments, private hostels and small apartment blocks house a very large share of the students and working people in every city, and almost all of them are still run on paper. Names go into a register at the front desk. Rent arrives by UPI transfer or in cash. The record of a payment is a screenshot sitting on somebody's phone. A tenant who has paid holds no receipt their landlord has ever acknowledged, and a landlord with six rooms usually cannot say with confidence who is behind this month.
#P TenantPro was built to replace that arrangement. It has two sides: a landlord application and a tenant portal, both served from one React Native codebase running on Expo SDK 54, talking to a Node and Express API over MySQL. The system is deployed and in use. At the commit documented here it comes to 38,536 lines across 124 source files, exposes 87 API endpoints over 22 database tables, and presents 28 screens.
#P One decision shapes the rest of the design. Money never passes through the platform. Rent goes straight from the tenant to the landlord, either by UPI into the landlord's own address or through a payment gateway connected to the landlord's own merchant account. That keeps the operator outside the Reserve Bank of India's payment-aggregator perimeter, which carries an authorisation requirement and a net-worth floor in the tens of crores, and it means the platform bills nobody and holds nobody's funds. The catch is that an application which does not touch the money also cannot see it. So every payment starts life as a claim and stays one until a second party vouches for it: the landlord tapping Confirm, or the gateway answering a direct query from the server. Both routes end in the same settlement function, so they cannot disagree about when a month has cleared.
#P The build carries 457 assertions across eight test suites. Of those, 168 run end to end against a live server and a real MySQL instance, and 71 drive the compiled application inside a browser engine. Those runs found five defects the unit tests had missed, one of them a cross-account authorisation hole. All five are fixed. This report sets out the architecture, the features as they actually stand, the payment and session designs, how the system was tested, and what has been identified but not built.
#P Keywords: Rent Management, Property Management System, Tenant Portal, Direct Payment Model, Payment Aggregator Regulation, UPI, Razorpay Payment Links, Refresh Token Rotation, React Native, Expo, Node.js, MySQL, End-to-End Testing.

#CH CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION
#S 1.1 Overview
#P Rented accommodation carries a large part of the urban housing load in India. Paying guest establishments, private hostels and small apartment blocks of anywhere between two and twenty rooms are where most students and most young working people live. Nearly all of these properties belong to individuals rather than to companies, and nearly all of them are managed without software of any kind.
#P A typical property runs out of a register. Names are written in when somebody moves in and struck out when they leave. Rent comes by UPI or in cash, and the only trace of it is a transaction screenshot the tenant forwards over WhatsApp. Deposits are remembered rather than written down. Maintenance complaints arrive as phone calls and are either dealt with or forgotten, with nothing recorded either way. None of this is a problem at one property with four rooms. It becomes one at two properties with twelve.
#P TenantPro replaces that patchwork with a single record both sides can see. The landlord gets a roster of properties, rooms and tenants, with due dates worked out from each tenancy's own anniversary. The tenant sees the same records from the other direction: what they owe, when it falls due, what they have paid, and which of it the landlord has acknowledged. The client is a React Native application shipped as an Android build. The server is a Node and Express API over MySQL, deployed and running.
#S 1.2 Problem Background
#P Anybody who has run a rented property for a year or two will recognise the problems with doing it by hand. Rent amounts get recorded inconsistently, or not at all. A payment made on the first and a payment made on the nineteenth look identical in the register once both have been ticked. When a tenant insists they have paid and the owner cannot find the entry, neither side has anything useful: the tenant has a screenshot proving a transfer happened, which says nothing about which month it was meant for.
#P Deposits are the worst version of the same thing. A deposit is collected at the start of a tenancy that might run two years and is settled at the end of it. In between, the only record is a figure in the owner's head or a line in a notebook that has since been thrown out. Arguments at move-out are common, and the tenant usually loses them, because the tenant has no record at all.
#P On the owner's side, what limits growth is the lack of a consolidated view. One property can be held in your head. Three cannot, and the first thing to go is a reliable answer to the simplest question in the business: who has not paid. Reminders then go either to everybody, which irritates the people who have already paid, or to nobody, which is why collection slips.
#S 1.3 Motivation
#P The idea came from watching both sides fail at the same task for opposite reasons. Owners described losing the first week of every month to chasing payments they could not confirm. Tenants described paying on time and then being asked again a week later with no way to prove the first payment had landed.
#P The surrounding conditions have also changed a great deal in the last five years. UPI has made digital payment ordinary rather than exceptional, so the movement of money is already solved and does not need reinventing. Cloud hosting is cheap. Practically every tenant carries a smartphone. What is missing is not a way to pay but a shared record of what the payment was for.
#P Looking at the applications already in this space showed a consistent split. The simpler ones are digital notebooks with no tenant-facing side, which leaves the tenant exactly where the paper register left them. The more ambitious ones fix the record problem by routing the rent through their own account and settling it on to the landlord afterwards. That solves visibility by creating a regulatory problem, and it also means the owner's rent spends time in somebody else's account. Neither approach suited the situation this project set out to serve.
#S 1.4 Objectives of the Project
#P The project was shaped around a set of objectives.
#N To replace manual record keeping with a structured digital record of properties, rooms, tenants, tenancies, rent, deposits and payments, held in a relational database instead of a register.
#N To give the tenant a real view of that record, so that the person paying the rent can see what is owed, what has been paid and what the landlord has formally acknowledged.
#N To work out each tenancy's due date from its own start date rather than from a shared calendar month, so that somebody who moved in on the nineteenth is due on the nineteenth.
#N To make digital payment the ordinary route, both by UPI straight into the landlord's account and through a gateway connected to the landlord's own merchant account.
#N To keep the platform out of the flow of funds completely, so that it never holds or transmits a tenant's money and does not fall inside the regulatory perimeter that applies to payment aggregators.
#N To make sure no payment is ever treated as settled on the payer's own say-so, by requiring confirmation from the landlord or the gateway before a rent cycle moves forward.
#N To send rent reminders automatically on the channels tenants actually use, and to give maintenance complaints a shared thread so that they are recorded rather than remembered.
#N To build a session design in which a session can actually be ended, so that a stolen phone, a tenant who has moved out or a password change has some real effect.
#N To test the result by driving every endpoint against a real database and the compiled application against a real browser engine, instead of relying on unit tests over mocks.
#S 1.5 Scope of the Project
#P The scope covers the operational life of an independently owned rental property. That means registering a landlord account, creating properties and rooms, bringing tenants on board either by direct entry or by an invite code they redeem, recording rent and deposit terms, generating due dates, sending reminders, accepting and confirming payments, keeping a searchable ledger, running maintenance threads, holding identity documents, computing a payment-behaviour score both parties can see, recording notice periods and leave dates, and giving the tenant a portal where all of it is visible from their side.
#P Some things were deliberately left out. The system does not hold or move money and is not an escrow. It does not draft, store or register rental agreements, and makes no claim on the legal side of a tenancy. It is not a listing marketplace and does no brokerage. There are no accounting exports and no tax filing. Expense and vendor management are outside the present scope. And the client is an Android application; no iOS build has been made.

Chapter 3 analyses the existing arrangement, lists its limitations, describes what is proposed instead, and reports a feasibility study run along four axes. #CH CHAPTER 2 — LITERATURE STUDY
#S 2.1 Introduction
#P A project like this starts by looking at what already exists. Three bodies of material mattered here: the informal practices that currently run the independent rental sector, the digital applications built to replace them, and the regulatory and security literature that constrains how anybody may build in this space. The third turned out to matter more than the second, because the central architectural decision in this project follows directly from it.
#S 2.2 Review of Traditional Manual Systems
#P There is a good deal of management literature on why small businesses stay on manual systems even when digital alternatives are cheap and available. The findings repeat. What the owner weighs is not the price of the software but the time and risk of moving to it. The owner is usually also the operator and has no spare capacity for a migration. And manual systems fail quietly, so nobody ever measures what they cost.
#P Work on small hospitality and rental businesses makes a related point: manual records understate actual losses. A missed payment, a partial payment nobody followed up, a deposit returned in full when deductions were due — none of these show up as losses anywhere, because there is no record to compare them against. The owner's sense of the business is therefore reliably rosier than its accounts would be, which weakens the case for changing anything.
#P The useful conclusion for this project was not that owners are being irrational. It was that any replacement has to be usable on day one without a migration project, and has to show its value in a way the owner can see. Several decisions later in the design follow from that, in particular the choice to let a landlord run the whole system with no payment gateway connected and still get the benefit of the record.
#S 2.3 Review of Existing Digital Rental Applications
#P A fair number of applications now target the Indian rental market. They range from simple mobile apps built by individual developers, through mid-tier rent-collection products, up to full property management suites aimed at organised co-living operators. Reviewing them showed a split that runs right through the category.
#P The simpler applications are digital registers. They give the owner somewhere to type what they would otherwise have written, which is a genuine improvement, but there is no tenant-facing side at all. The tenant ends up where they started, with no acknowledged receipt and no view of their own history. Several also model a property as a flat list of tenants, with no notion of a property holding rooms holding occupants, which makes them unusable past one small building.
#P The more capable products do have a tenant side, and they solve the confirmation problem convincingly, because they collect the rent themselves. The tenant pays the platform, the platform marks the payment received and then settles the money on to the landlord. From a software point of view this is clean. It also places the platform squarely inside the regulatory perimeter discussed in the next section, and it means the landlord's rent passes through an account that is not theirs on the way to them. Several of the owners we spoke to during this project objected to that without being prompted.

#S 2.4 Payment Models on Two-Sided Platforms
#P Any platform sitting between a payer and a payee has three structural options, and choosing between them is a regulatory decision before it is a technical one.
#P Under the aggregator model the platform receives the customer's money into its own account and then pays the merchant. The Reserve Bank of India's directions on payment aggregators make this a regulated activity. An aggregator cannot keep customer funds in its ordinary account. The money has to sit in a segregated escrow with a scheduled commercial bank, it earns the platform no interest, and it has to be settled out within prescribed timelines. Getting the authorisation at all means clearing a net-worth floor in the tens of crores. Ride-hailing platforms are the familiar example.
#P Under split settlement the platform does not hold the money itself. Instead it instructs a licensed gateway to split an incoming payment between a platform account and a linked sub-merchant account. The gateway holds the licence and runs the know-your-customer checks on the sub-merchants. Razorpay Route is the common implementation in India. This takes the onboarding burden off the platform and is attractive at scale, but funds still pass through a platform-controlled account, and the 2025 directions restrict how such arrangements may be put together.
#P Under the direct model the platform never touches the money. The payer pays the payee. All the platform does is present the right amount, the right destination and a reference, and record what happened afterwards. Namma Yatri is the best-documented Indian precedent: a ride-hailing service where the fare goes straight from rider to driver and the platform takes no commission, sustaining itself some other way. TenantPro sits here.
#TBL Table 2.2 — Structural Options for Moving Money on a Platform
|Model|Who holds the funds|Authorisation required|Example
|Aggregator|The platform, in a ring-fenced escrow at a scheduled commercial bank|RBI payment aggregator licence|Uber, Ola
|Split settlement|The gateway, settling to linked sub-merchant accounts|Held by the gateway, not the platform|Razorpay Route
|Direct|Nobody; the payer pays the payee|None|Namma Yatri, TenantPro
#S 2.5 Review of Token-Based Session Management
#P The security literature on sessions for mobile clients turns on one trade-off: stateless tokens against stateful ones. A signed access token of the JSON Web Token family is checked by its signature alone, with no database lookup. That is what makes it fast and what lets it scale. The price is that nothing can take it back. A stolen device, a tenancy that has ended, a password somebody just changed — none of these affect a token already in circulation. It runs until it expires.
#P Current practice, set out in the OAuth 2.0 security guidance, pairs a short-lived access token with a long-lived refresh token held on the server, so that revocation has somewhere to act. Two further properties are recommended. Rotation: presenting a refresh token spends it and returns a replacement, so a long-lived secret is never sent across the network over and over. And reuse detection: since rotation means the honest client is always holding the newest token, a token that has already been spent turning up again implies that a copy exists somewhere it should not, and the safe answer is to end every session on that account.
#P The literature is equally clear that the client half is where this usually goes wrong. A client that signs the user out on any authentication-looking error will sign them out on a dropped connection. A client that fires several requests at once on a cold start, all failing on the same expired token, will issue several refreshes, and all but the first will replay a spent token — which a correctly implemented server reads as theft. Both failure modes were designed against here and both are covered by tests.
#S 2.6 Gap Analysis
#P Putting the three strands together, the gap is reasonably clear. Manual systems still dominate the independent rental sector despite being demonstrably lossy, because the cost of switching is real and the losses are invisible. The simpler digital applications cut the owner's effort but leave the tenant where they were, which means the dispute the system most needs to prevent is the one thing it does nothing about. The more capable platforms do give the tenant a real view, but they get there by taking custody of the rent, which is regulated and is in any case unattractive to the owners this project is for.
#P What nobody is offering is a system that gives both sides a shared, authoritative record of a tenancy while leaving the money exactly where it already flows, from the tenant's account into the landlord's. That is the gap TenantPro fills, and the confirmation model described in Chapter 4 is how it stays accurate about payments it is deliberately unable to watch.

#CH CHAPTER 3 — SYSTEM ANALYSIS
#S 3.1 Existing System
#P The arrangement we found across the properties studied for this project is a physical register at the property, a notebook or spreadsheet with the owner, a WhatsApp thread per tenant, and the owner's memory holding the rest together. New tenants go into the register with a phone number and a room. Rent terms are agreed verbally, or in a short written agreement that is then filed and never looked at again. Payment is by UPI or cash. A UPI payment produces a confirmation in the tenant's own banking app, which they screenshot and send across; a cash payment produces nothing at all.
#P Reminders go out by hand, usually in a burst at the start of the month, and usually to everybody, because working out who has already paid takes longer than just sending the message. Maintenance complaints come in by phone or WhatsApp and are tracked, if at all, in the same thread as everything else. Deposits are recorded once at the beginning and recalled at the end. Notice periods are settled in conversation.
#P At best a diligent owner keeps all this running with the occasional error. More often entries are missed, payments get chased twice, a deposit figure is disputed at move-out, and a complaint made three weeks ago has been forgotten by both parties.
#S 3.2 Limitations of the Existing System
#P Accuracy goes first. Every figure is copied by hand at least once and some are never written down at all. Timeliness goes next: the owner's picture of who has paid lags reality by however long it takes to reconcile a pile of screenshots against the register. Evidence is one-sided, since the tenant holds proof that a transfer happened but no proof of what it settled, while the owner holds a register entry the tenant has never seen.
#P Scalability does not degrade gradually. It fails at a threshold, somewhere around the second or third property, which is exactly where an owner would otherwise expand. There is no accountability, because nothing records who entered what or when. And the tenant, who is after all the paying customer of the business, gets no interface whatsoever. That last point is the root of most of the disputes the arrangement produces.
#S 3.3 Proposed System
#P The proposed system swaps the register and the notebook for a relational record, and puts an application over it for each party. Properties hold rooms, rooms hold tenancies, and each tenancy carries its own rent, deposit, start date and split across occupants. Due dates come from the tenancy's own start date, so a tenant who moved in on the nineteenth is due on the nineteenth and not on the first.
#P Payments are recorded against the due date they settle, captured at the moment the payment is declared. A payment enters as a claim and becomes real only when a second party vouches for it. Where the landlord has connected a gateway, the second party is the gateway, asked directly by the server using that landlord's own credentials. Where they have not, it is the landlord, who confirms or rejects with a reason the tenant can read. Either way the same settlement function moves the tenancy on.
#P The tenant portal is a parallel application, not a stripped-down copy of the landlord's. It answers a different question, and it is reached through a separate account type with its own table and its own token role. Reminders go out automatically across email, push, SMS and WhatsApp. Maintenance requests become threads the two parties share, with status changes recorded in sequence alongside what was said. A payment-behaviour score is computed once and shown identically to both sides, so neither is arguing with a number the other cannot see.
#S 3.4 Advantages of the Proposed System
#P Figures are computed rather than copied, so the arithmetic stops being a source of error. The landlord's view of collection is built from the same rows the tenant's view is built from, which removes the lag. Confirmation becomes an explicit recorded act instead of an assumption, so both sides hold the same evidence. A second and third property cost nothing but data entry. And every payment, confirmation, rejection and status change is a row with a timestamp on it, which is as much accountability as a system of this size needs.

|Aspect|Existing system|Proposed system
|Record of tenancy|Register entry and memory|Relational record, visible to both parties
|Due date|Shared calendar month, applied loosely|Derived from each tenancy's own start date
|Proof of payment|Tenant's screenshot of a transfer|Confirmed payment row carrying the due date it settled
|Who confirms|Nobody; assumed on sight of a screenshot|Landlord, or the gateway asked server-to-server
|Reminders|Sent by hand, usually to everybody|Automated daily job, addressed to those actually due
|Deposit record|Remembered|Stored, and shown on the tenant's own screen

#S 3.5 Feasibility Study
#P Feasibility was examined along four axes. The fourth is not one of the standard three, but it decided more about this project than the others did.
#SS 3.5.1 Technical Feasibility
#P Everything the system needs already exists and is well documented. React Native with Expo gives a single codebase targeting Android, with over-the-air updates for JavaScript changes. Node and Express over MySQL is an unremarkable server stack. Payment links, transactional email, push notifications and messaging are all reachable as ordinary HTTPS APIs. Nothing here had to be built from first principles. One technical constraint did shape the design and is picked up again in Chapter 4: native modules cannot be delivered over the air, so any feature needing a native software development kit would hold back the release cadence of everything queued behind it.
#SS 3.5.2 Economic Feasibility
#P Running costs are small. Hosting for the API and the database, transactional email, push delivery and media storage all sit within free or low tiers at the scale of a few properties, and they scale gracefully after that. Because the platform processes no payments, it carries no payment-processing cost of its own. Where a landlord connects a gateway, the gateway's fee is charged by the gateway to that landlord against their own merchant account; the platform is not a party to it and bills nothing. The one recurring cost identified on the roadmap is a verified sending domain for transactional mail, at roughly a thousand rupees a year.
#SS 3.5.3 Operational Feasibility
#P This is the axis we examined most carefully, because the people who will use the system are not necessarily comfortable with software. Two design consequences came out of it. The first is that the system has to be fully useful with no gateway connected, since gateway onboarding runs through a third party's verification process and takes days; a landlord who cannot use the product until that finishes is not coming back. The second is that any screen asking for a commitment has to state its cost and its prerequisites before the user starts, because a landlord who expects five minutes and runs into a week concludes the application is broken rather than that the gateway is slow.
#SS 3.5.4 Regulatory Feasibility
#P The regulatory axis settled the architecture. Collecting rent centrally and paying it out is payment aggregation under the Reserve Bank of India's directions, with the escrow obligations and the net-worth floor described in Chapter 2. A student project cannot meet those conditions. Neither can the individual landlords this system serves. So it was designed so that the question never comes up: the money moves straight from the tenant to the landlord, by UPI or through the landlord's own merchant account, and the platform neither receives it nor tells anybody to disburse it.
#TBL Table 3.2 — Feasibility Analysis Summary
|Axis|Finding|Consequence for the design
|Technical|All components mature and documented|Native modules avoided so that updates ship over the air
|Economic|Low fixed cost; no payment-processing cost borne by the platform|Platform bills nobody; gateway fees are the landlord's own
|Operational|Users are not necessarily technical; onboarding friction is fatal|System fully usable with no gateway connected
|Regulatory|Central collection would be payment aggregation|Direct tenant-to-landlord payment; platform outside the flow of funds

#CH CHAPTER 4 — SYSTEM DESIGN AND ARCHITECTURE
#S 4.1 System Architecture
#P TenantPro uses a three-tier design. The presentation tier is a React Native client that serves the landlord application and the tenant portal from one codebase. The application tier is a Node and Express API organised as routers, each with its role guard mounted once at the top. The data tier is MySQL, with 22 tables covering accounts, properties, rooms, tenancies, payments, maintenance threads, documents, notifications and sessions.
#P Around the three tiers sit the supporting services: a transactional mail provider, push delivery, media storage for identity documents and maintenance photographs, and the payment gateway. The last one is drawn differently in the figure below, for a reason. Every piece of information about a tenancy goes through the server. No part of the payment does. The dashed band in the figure is the money path, and it touches the tenant and the landlord and nothing in between.

#S 4.3 Activity Diagrams
#P Two activity diagrams are given. The first takes in the system as a whole; the second zooms into the one flow that carries the most design weight.
#P Figure 4.3 follows a tenancy from end to end across three swimlanes: what the landlord does, what the system does on its own, and what the tenant does. Reading down the lanes shows how little of the work sits with any one party. The landlord registers, builds the portfolio and decides on a join request. The tenant joins, looks at what is owed, pays and raises tickets. In between, the system issues the session, derives the due date from the tenancy start date, runs the morning reminder job, settles payments and computes a leave date.
#P Three branches in the diagram end early rather than carrying on, and that is deliberate. A join request can be declined. Both are ordinary outcomes, not errors, and a diagram that showed only the happy path would be describing a different system from the one that was built.

#S 4.5 Client Architecture and the View-Model
#P One React Native codebase serves both roles. Rather than spreading presentation logic across screens, the application runs the current state and the server payload through a single pure function that produces a view-model. A screen then holds layout and very little else.
#P Two things follow. Because the function is pure and needs no device, the behaviour of the interface can be asserted directly, and one of the suites in Chapter 6 does exactly that. And two screens showing the same fact cannot drift apart, since both read the same derived value instead of each working it out for itself.
#P The delivery mechanism imposed a constraint that shaped what got built. Updates reach an installed client through the Expo updates mechanism, which can swap out JavaScript but not native modules. Anything depending on a native software development kit can only go out in a full store release, and would hold back every other change waiting behind it. Features of that kind were turned down wherever a design existed that ships like everything else. The payment integration is the clearest case and is covered in section 4.7.
#S 4.6 API and Authorisation Layer
#P The API is organised as routers, each with its role guard mounted once at the top instead of repeated in every handler. Landlord and tenant accounts live in separate tables with their own auto-increments, so knowing who somebody is does not by itself settle what they may do. A tenant token on a landlord route is turned away by the guard, and tenant handlers scope every query through the link from the tenant account to the tenancy record.
#P Ownership checks are a second and separate layer. Every read is scoped by owner, but a write that accepts an identifier from the request body needs an explicit check that the row it names belongs to the caller. Those checks now live in one helper module rather than being written out at each site. There is a reason for that, recorded in Chapter 6: when they were written out individually, one of three endpoints was missed.
#P Schema changes apply themselves at boot, and apply safely more than once. The schema file runs its table creation statements conditionally on every start, and column-level migrations run behind existence guards. No deploy has needed a manual migration step.
#S 4.7 Payment and Settlement Design
#P There are two payment routes and both are direct.
#P The first is UPI. The tenant's screen carries the landlord's own virtual payment address, the exact amount and a generated reference, encoded as a scannable code and as an intent that hands off to whichever UPI application the tenant already uses. The money goes from the tenant's bank to the landlord's. Nobody pays a fee, including the platform.
#P The second is a payment gateway. A landlord can connect their own Razorpay merchant account by entering their key and secret, which are checked against the gateway before being stored and are then sealed with authenticated encryption and never returned to any client. When a tenant pays this way the server creates a payment link against that landlord's own credentials; the tenant pays on the gateway's hosted page and the money settles to that landlord. The gateway charges its fee to the landlord through the landlord's own account. The platform bills nothing, because it is party to nothing.
#P Payment links were chosen over the gateway's React Native software development kit for the reason given in section 4.5. The kit is a native module. A link is plain REST over HTTPS, so the payment feature ships over the air like every other change.
#P The confirmation model ties the two routes together. A payment enters as a declared row, which is a claim and clears nothing. It becomes real when a second party vouches for it: the landlord tapping Confirm, or the gateway being asked outright, server to server, with that landlord's credentials, and answering that the link was paid. Both paths end in the same settlement function, which flips the status and moves the tenancy's next due date forward inside one transaction. Write that twice and the two copies will eventually disagree about when a month has cleared, so it is written once.
#P The gateway gets polled rather than trusted to call back. A webhook would mean every landlord configuring a URL and a shared secret in a dashboard, and most of them would never finish doing it. The signed webhook path exists and is still supported; it is just the secondary route.
#S 4.8 Session and Authentication Design
#P The session design went through three versions. The sequence is worth recording, because each of the first two fixed something real and created the next problem while doing it.
#TBL Table 4.1 — Evolution of the Session Design
|Stage|Access token|Can be revoked|Failure it solved|Failure it left
|Original|7 days, fixed|No|—|Weekly logout for every user
|Sliding|30 days, renewed on use|No|The weekly logout|Nothing could be cancelled
|Current|2 hours plus a refresh token|Yes|Revocation|Lag of up to two hours on the access path
#P Raising the lifetime from seven days to thirty did not stop people being logged out. It made it happen monthly. A fixed lifetime counts from the last sign-in, which says nothing about whether somebody is still using the application. Open the app every morning and you were thrown out on day thirty, exactly like somebody who had not opened it since February. So the server now re-issues the access token on any authenticated request once it is past half its life, handing it back on a response header the client picks up. The new token's lifetime comes from the old one's own issue and expiry claims rather than from a constant, which is what lets a guest session keep its own length.
#P A session is now a database row as well as a signature. The row holds a refresh token stored as a SHA-256 hash rather than as the token itself, since the only thing ever done with it is a comparison. Three properties follow. Rotation: spending a refresh token gets you a replacement, so a sixty-day secret is never sent repeatedly over the network. Reuse detection: the honest client always holds the newest token, so a replay of a spent one says a copy is sitting somewhere else, and every session on that account is revoked. Revocation on password reset: changing a password now ends every other session, which is what users had assumed all along that it did.
#P Revocation is immediate on the refresh path. On the access path it lags by at most the access token's two-hour life. Closing that last gap would mean a database read on every authenticated request, which is a cost almost nobody pays. Two hours where it used to be thirty days is the gain.
#S 4.9 Database Design
#P The schema holds 22 tables and follows the entity structure closely. Landlord accounts and tenant accounts are separate tables, which is what lets identity and authorisation be told apart at the API layer. Properties belong to landlords, rooms belong to properties, and tenancies join a tenant record to a room with their own rent, deposit, split and dates. Payments reference the tenancy and carry the due date they settled, and that last field matters more than it looks. Without it, "was this on time?" has no answer after the fact, because confirming a payment moves the next due date forward and overwrites the only evidence. Its earlier absence is why the behaviour score once reported that no tenant had ever missed a payment.
#TBL Table 4.2 — Principal Tables and Their Role
|Table group|Holds|Notes
|Accounts|Landlord accounts, tenant accounts|Separate tables with independent keys
|Portfolio|Properties, rooms|Rooms carry rent, capacity and notification preferences
|Tenancy|Tenant records, assignments, notice periods|Due dates derived from each tenancy's own start date
|Payments|Declared, confirmed and rejected payments|Each row stores the due date it settled
|Maintenance|Requests and thread messages|Status transitions stored as events in the thread
|Documents|Identity proofs and maintenance photographs|Stored externally; previews served under short-lived tokens
|Notifications|Alerts, delivery records, seen markers|Seen marker is stamped by the server, not the device

#S 5.3 Programming Platform Features
#P React Native with Expo gives a single JavaScript codebase that runs on the device while still letting most changes go out without a store release. That second property is not a convenience. It is a constraint, and it shaped the feature set in the way described in Chapter 4.
#P Node with Express was picked for the server because JavaScript on both sides removes a translation layer between client and server models, and because the Express router model maps neatly onto the role-guard design. MySQL was picked over a document store because this data is strongly relational: a payment means nothing except in relation to a tenancy, which means nothing except in relation to a room and a property.
#P External integrations are reached through documented REST APIs with explicit error handling. One point of care is worth flagging here and comes up again in Chapter 6. A request that never reached a third party is a different event from one that reached it and was refused, and the two must not be reported to the user in the same words.
#S 5.4 Functional Requirements
#P The system shall let a landlord register, sign in and recover a forgotten password. It shall let them create properties and rooms, with per-room rent, capacity, occupancy status and notification preferences. Tenants shall be creatable directly or able to join by redeeming an invite code, with the landlord accepting or declining and optionally assigning a room at the same time.
#P It shall work out each tenancy's due date from its own start date and maintain every tenant's due state. It shall let a tenant declare a payment, and shall record that payment as a claim carrying the due date it is meant to settle. It shall let a landlord confirm or reject a claim, with a rejection reason the tenant can read. Where a landlord has connected a gateway, it shall create a payment link against that landlord's credentials and shall confirm the payment by asking the gateway rather than by trusting the client.
#P It shall keep a searchable ledger of confirmed payments, narrowable to one property. It shall send rent reminders daily on the configured channels. It shall provide maintenance threads shared between landlord and tenant, with status transitions recorded in sequence. It shall accept identity documents and serve previews under short-lived tokens. It shall compute a payment-behaviour score from the same code for both parties and itemise its factors. It shall record notice periods and compute leave dates on the server. And it shall give the tenant a portal showing their dues, history, receipts, deposit, tenure and score.
#S 5.5 Non-Functional Requirements
#P Usability comes first, because the users are not necessarily technical. Screens are expected to state the cost and the prerequisites of an action before the user commits to it, and error messages are expected to tell a network failure, a refusal by a third party and a mistake by the user apart from one another.
#P Security requires third-party credentials to be stored encrypted and never returned to a client, refresh tokens to be stored only as hashes, sessions to be revocable, and authorisation to be enforced on the server for every write that accepts an identifier from the client. Correctness requires that a payment is never treated as settled on the payer's assertion alone, and that a status change and a due-date advance happen inside one transaction.
#P Reliability requires that a passing network failure never signs a user out and is never reported as a rejected credential. Maintainability requires that behaviour shared between the two applications is implemented once. Portability requires that schema changes apply themselves at boot, so that a deploy is a code deploy and nothing more.

#CH CHAPTER 6 — IMPLEMENTATION
#P Implementation went module by module, each module being a functional area with its own routes on the server and its own derived state on the client. They are described below roughly in the order a landlord and a tenant run into them.
#S 6.1 Authentication and Session Module
#P This module registers accounts, authenticates them, issues sessions and ends them. Landlord and tenant accounts sit in separate tables, and the token issued to each carries a role the API's guards act on. Sign-in can also go through a federated Google identity.
#P A session is a short-lived access token plus a refresh token held as a hash in a sessions table. The access token is renewed quietly on any authenticated request once it passes half its life. The refresh token rotates every time it is used, and presenting one that has already been spent revokes every session on the account. Signing out revokes the row with a recorded reason, and a password reset revokes every other session.
#P The client half needed the most care. A failed request triggers at most one refresh and one replay, and only a refusal from the refresh endpoint itself ends the session; a server fault, a timeout or a dead connection does not. Parallel refreshes all share one in-flight promise, so an application firing eight requests on a cold start issues a single refresh instead of eight. Without that, seven of the eight would replay a token the first had already spent, the server would correctly read the replay as theft, and the feature built to stop lockouts would have been causing them.
#S 6.2 Property, Room and Tenant Management Module
#P This is where the landlord describes the shape of the business. Properties get a name, a type and an address. Rooms get a number, rent, capacity and their own notification preferences. A room's occupancy status follows its occupants on its own.
#P Tenants can be created directly by the landlord or can join by redeeming an invite code, in which case the landlord sees the request with the tenant's details and any identity documents before accepting it. A tenant is allowed to exist without a room, and that state is reachable from the roster rather than tucked out of sight, because somebody added but not yet placed is an ordinary situation and not an error.
#P Every write in this module that takes a room identifier from the request body checks that the room belongs to the caller. That check sits in one shared helper. It did not always, and section 6.10 says what came of it.
#S 6.3 Rent Cycle and Due Date Module
#P Each tenancy carries its own start date, rent and split. The due date is computed from the tenancy's own anniversary rather than from a shared calendar month, which is what lets a tenant who moved in on the nineteenth be due on the nineteenth. The module keeps every tenant's due state and exposes it as the chip shown on the roster: due in so many days, so many days late, or a leaving date where notice has been given.
#P Advancing the due date is not done here. It happens only inside the settlement transaction described in section 6.4, so a due date can never move without a payment having settled, and a payment can never settle without the due date moving.
#S 6.4 Payment and Confirmation Module
#P A payment is created as a declared row carrying the amount, a generated reference, the method, and the due date it is meant to settle. In that state it is a claim and clears nothing.
#P Confirmation comes from one of two places. The landlord can confirm or reject the claim from a queue showing the context needed to decide, and a rejection carries a reason the tenant reads. Or, where the payment went through a connected gateway, the server asks the gateway directly using that landlord's credentials and settles on the gateway's own answer. Both call the same settlement function, which flips the status and moves the tenancy's next due date forward in one transaction.
#P On the tenant's side the distinction is made visible rather than hidden. A claim still waiting on the landlord shows in amber as awaiting confirmation, not in the green kept for money that has arrived. Show the two alike and a tenant will believe their month is clear when it is not.
#S 6.5 Payment Gateway Connection Module
#P This module lets a landlord connect their own Razorpay merchant account. The sheet that does it states the gateway's fee right next to the offer, because describing one without the other is how somebody switches on a percentage fee without noticing. It also sets out what the gateway is going to ask for before the landlord starts: a permanent account number, a bank account, one business proof, and realistically two to five working days. It adds that test keys work straight away and that tenants can carry on paying by UPI the whole time.
#P Credentials are checked against the gateway before anything is stored, so a mistyped secret is caught while the landlord still has the form in front of them. What gets stored is sealed with AES-256-GCM and never returned to any client. A failure to reach the gateway is reported as a network failure and not as a rejected credential; the word "rejected" is kept for the gateway actually saying no.
#S 6.6 Notification and Reminder Module
#P A scheduled job runs each morning and sends rent reminders to the tenants who are actually due, across email, push, SMS and WhatsApp according to the room's preferences, with every channel phrasing the same day the same way. Beyond reminders, the module raises alerts for a landlord's reply on a maintenance thread, a payment confirmed or rejected, and the outcome of a join request.
#P The alert indicator works off the same payload the screen renders rather than a separate unread counter that could drift away from it. A seen marker tells an alert that is news apart from one that is merely still true, and the server stamps that marker, so a phone with the wrong clock cannot silence its own alerts.
#S 6.7 Maintenance Request Module
#P A tenant raises a request with a category, a priority and a photograph if they have one, then follows it in a thread shared with the landlord. Status changes land in that thread as events, in order with whatever was said around them, so the history of a repair reads as one story instead of a conversation plus a separate status field.
#S 6.8 Tenant Behaviour Score Module
#P The module computes a payment-behaviour score from the tenant's record of on-time, late and missed payments. One piece of code produces it and both sides see the same number, which is the whole point: a tenant looking at a different figure from the one their landlord sees cannot argue with either. The factors are listed out instead of being boiled down to a single dial, and where there is too little history to say anything, the screen says so. The alternative is a needle parked at zero, which reads like a verdict the tenant has earned.
#S 6.9 Tenant Portal Module
#P The tenant portal is a parallel application rather than a cut-down copy of the landlord's. Its home screen carries the rent card with the amount, the property and room, the exact due date and a countdown, and underneath it the deposit held and the tenure. Both of those are things tenants routinely cannot remember and have no other record of.
#P The module also covers guest accounts for tenants with no email address, identified by a code with a stay window enforced on the server; turning a guest into a full account; joining a property by scanning or typing an invite code; receipts with status, reference and any rejection reason; giving and withdrawing notice, with the real dates worked out and shown before anything is written; uploading and removing identity documents; and directions to the property where the landlord has pinned it.

#SS 6.10.1 Unit Testing
#P Unit tests cover the calculation-heavy and security-critical functions on their own: sealing and unsealing gateway credentials, hashing and comparing refresh tokens, the renewal arithmetic that reads a token's remaining lifetime out of its own claims, and the view-model function that turns server state into what the screens render.
#SS 6.10.2 Integration Testing
#P Integration tests run the controllers with their dependencies stubbed at the boundary rather than inside. The gateway suite is the clearest example: it runs the real controller code against a stubbed network, which is how the difference between a refused credential and an unreachable host can be asserted directly instead of inferred.
#SS 6.10.3 End-to-End Testing
#P The end-to-end suites run against a live server and a real MySQL instance seeded by a deterministic generator. That generator deliberately produces the rows that break screens and never turn up when you want them: a tenant with no room, a guest with no email, a guest whose stay has already ended, rent overdue and due today and upcoming and never set at all, a payment declared and undecided, another rejected with a reason, maintenance threads where the landlord spoke last and others where the tenant did, a tenant who has given notice, and a property with no rooms in it. The pseudo-random number generator is seeded, so a failing run can be reproduced exactly.
#P The refresh suite goes further and reads the sessions table directly, so claims about rotation, reuse detection and revocation are checked against stored rows rather than against the API's account of itself. During one live browser run a session was driven through three access-token expiries without the user being signed out. Signing out through the interface afterwards was confirmed to have written a revocation reason into the row and to have made the token unusable.
#SS 6.10.4 Browser-Driven User Interface Testing
#P The user interface suite runs the same JavaScript the phone runs: the same view-model, the same screens, the same request client and interceptors, laid out for the browser by the web renderer an Expo web export produces, and driven through Chromium. What it cannot reach is anything behind a native module, so the camera, the image picker, push delivery and the UPI intent hand-off are all out of its scope. For those it prints a skip rather than a pass, so a green run never claims coverage it does not have.
#P Running the application in a desktop browser is not a substitute for a device and is not claimed as one here. It is, though, the only automated way available in this project's environment to assert that a real screen renders a real row correctly, and it found defects no unit test had.
#SS 6.10.5 Defects Found
#P Driving every endpoint against a real database, and the application against a real browser, turned up five defects the unit tests had missed.

|Defect|Severity|Status

#P The first one is worth dwelling on, since it is why ownership checks were centralised. Every read in the system is scoped by owner, so the second landlord never saw the intruding row. The leak was that the first landlord's own tenant list then displayed a stranger's property name and room number. We found it by filing one landlord's tenant into another's room through the API and getting a cheerful success response back.
#P Two more defects turned up in the test harnesses themselves, which is worth putting on the record. The first browser run never got past the permissions prompt, and because its assertions were phrased as the absence of a string that the prompt also happened to lack, half the suite passed while testing nothing at all. Separately, the failure reporter crashed on a failure whose context was undefined and took down the run it was meant to be reporting. The lessons are to assert on what should be there rather than on what should not, and to keep the failure reporter the dullest code in the file.

#CH CHAPTER 8 — CONCLUSION AND FUTURE ENHANCEMENT
#S 8.1 Conclusion
#P This project set out to deal with a specific and long-standing gap in the independent rental sector: manual record keeping still dominates, and because of it there is no shared, authoritative account of what a tenant owes and what they have paid. What we built to address it is a working two-sided application, deployed and in use, made of a React Native client and a Node and Express API over MySQL, running to 38,536 lines across 124 source files, 87 endpoints, 22 tables and 28 screens.
#P What separates it from the alternatives reviewed in Chapter 2 is that the platform never touches the money. Rent goes straight from the tenant to the landlord, by UPI or through the landlord's own merchant account, and the platform only states the correct amount and destination and records what happened. That keeps the operator outside the payment-aggregator perimeter, costs both parties nothing, and leaves settlement where the landlords we spoke to wanted it. The price is that the system cannot observe money it does not handle, and the confirmation model exists to pay that price honestly rather than to pretend it does not apply.
#P The verification work produced the most useful findings of the project. Exercising all 87 endpoints against a real database and the compiled application against a real browser engine turned up five defects that unit tests over mocks had not, among them a cross-account authorisation hole that let one landlord's tenant be written into another landlord's room. Two further defects were in the test harnesses. The experience supports a conclusion that is well established in the literature and still easy to ignore in a student project: tests that assert on the absence of a string, and systems only ever tested against their own mocks, give false confidence.
#P More broadly, the project shows that an independent landlord does not have to choose between a paper register and handing custody of their rent to a platform. A system can give both sides a complete, shared record while leaving the money exactly where it already flows.
#S 8.2 Limitations of the Present Work
#P These are set out plainly, since each one follows from a design decision rather than from an oversight. The application cannot observe money. It never sees a bank balance, so a UPI payment stays a claim until the landlord confirms it, and for a landlord who has not connected a gateway the confirmation step is structural rather than incidental. Revocation lags by up to two hours on the access path, because closing that gap completely would mean a database lookup on every authenticated request.
#P Reuse detection can produce a false positive. A client that stores a new access token but fails to store the refresh token issued with it will present a spent token and lose every session. The client-side write is transactional, but a device that loses its storage halfway through would still land here. SMS is unconfigured, because phone verification needs a telecom registration that has not been done; email and push are both live. No iOS build has ever been produced, the bundle identifier is unset, and the project has only ever turned out Android artifacts.
#P Gateway onboarding is heavy. A landlord connecting Razorpay has to get through that gateway's own verification, realistically two to five working days, and although the application now says so before they start, it is still the biggest piece of friction in the product. Finally there is no automated regression gate. The suites exist and they pass, but nothing runs them on push, so verification stays a manual step somebody has to decide to take.
#S 8.3 Future Enhancement
#P Several directions have been identified. Some are small and specified; others are larger and need more thought.
#P The immediate items are well understood. Reply notifications on maintenance threads are incomplete: a tenant replying to a repair request currently notifies nobody, though the landlord-to-tenant direction already works. The landlord's own payment-entry path inserts the payment and updates the due date in two separate statements where the tenant-facing path is already transactional, and that asymmetry should go. The suites are reproducible and fast enough to run on every push, so wiring them into continuous integration is configuration work rather than development work. An iOS build needs a bundle identifier and a first run through the pipeline. And a landlord announcement feature, a one-to-many message to everyone in a property, was specified during the notification work and then never built, although the alert infrastructure that would carry it already exists.
#P The larger directions need more care. Split settlement through a gateway's linked-account facility would let landlords onboard from inside the application, with the gateway doing their verification, which would remove the onboarding friction named above. The cost is that funds would then pass through a platform account, and that is a change of regulatory category rather than of configuration. The 2025 directions restrict such arrangements in ways that call for professional advice rather than inference. Worth revisiting when the user base justifies it. Not before.
#P Monetisation, if it is ever pursued, should stay out of the money path. The Namma Yatri precedent applies here almost directly: a flat per-landlord subscription keeps the operator outside the payment flow, whereas a percentage of each transaction starts to look structurally like aggregation. There is a related legal nuance, which is that models of this kind rest on selling access to software rather than on setting the price of the underlying service. In TenantPro the landlord sets the rent, and that is a position worth holding on to deliberately.
#P Two smaller pieces of work are already identified on the messaging side. Transactional mail currently goes out from a consumer address through a provider's HTTPS interface; a verified sending domain with the usual authentication records would improve inbox placement a good deal, for about a thousand rupees a year. And phone verification needs an Indian telecom registration before any operator will carry template traffic.
