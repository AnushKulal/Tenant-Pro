# Rental record-keeping in independent tenancies — survey instrument

**Target population.** People currently renting a room or flat in an independent
property (paying guest accommodation, private hostel, or a small apartment block),
and people who let such property out. Urban India, with Bengaluru as the primary
recruitment base.

**Target sample.** 150–300 completed responses. 150 is enough to report
proportions with a margin of error around ±8 points; 300 brings that to about ±5.6.

**Expected completion time.** 4–6 minutes. This matters more than it sounds: a
form that takes longer than it promises is abandoned halfway, and partial responses
are worse than fewer responses.

---

## Design notes — read before editing any question

These are the rules the instrument was written to. Breaking one quietly breaks
the analysis.

**Behaviour before opinion.** Wherever a proposition can be tested by asking what
happened, the question asks what happened. "In the last 12 months, has there been
a disagreement with your landlord about rent or deposit?" is answerable and
checkable. "Do you think record-keeping is important?" is neither, and everyone
says yes.

**No agree/disagree batteries.** A column of statements all phrased positively
collects acquiescence — the tendency to agree — not opinion. Where an attitude is
genuinely needed, the question offers the competing positions side by side with
their real costs.

**The trade-off question is stated both ways.** Q16 is the one the project's
central claim rests on, and it is the easiest question in the instrument to rig.
It presents both arrangements with a genuine advantage and a genuine disadvantage
each. If it is ever edited to make one sound better, the answer stops meaning
anything.

**No priming.** Nothing in the form, the title, or the recruitment message says
what previous respondents chose, what the researcher expects, or that a product
exists. Respondents who know the desired answer supply it.

**Minimum personal data.** No name, no phone number, no email, no address, no
income. Not one of them is needed for any analysis below, and collecting personal
data without a purpose is exactly what the Digital Personal Data Protection Act,
2023 is aimed at. The only optional contact field is at the very end, after the
substantive questions, clearly marked optional, and used for nothing but a
follow-up interview if the respondent volunteers.

**Rent is asked in bands, not rupees.** An exact figure is more sensitive, less
reliably recalled, and no more useful once it is bucketed for analysis anyway.

## Form settings

| Setting | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Collect email addresses | **Off** | Not needed; its presence lowers completion and raises the data-protection burden |
| Limit to 1 response | **Off** | Requires sign-in, which excludes respondents and links identity to answers |
| Response receipts | Off | — |
| Progress bar | **On** | Reduces mid-form abandonment |
| Shuffle option order | **On for Q9, Q13, Q19** | Stops the first option being over-picked |
| Required | As marked below | Keep optional anything a respondent might reasonably refuse |

## Section 1 — Purpose and consent

> **Rental record-keeping in independent tenancies**
>
> This short survey is academic research for a postgraduate project. It asks how
> rent, deposits and maintenance are recorded in paying guest accommodation,
> hostels and small rented flats.
>
> It takes about five minutes. It does not ask for your name, phone number or
> address. Answers are analysed only in aggregate and are not shared with any
> landlord, tenant or company. You can stop at any point, and you can skip any
> question you would rather not answer.
>
> Proceeding to the next page indicates your consent to take part.

**Q1. Are you 18 or older?** *(Required — multiple choice)*
- Yes
- No → **end the form** with: "Thank you. This survey is open only to people aged 18 or over."

> *Analysis note: a screening question, not data. Minors cannot hold a tenancy in
> their own name under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and consent for research
> participation is a separate problem this project does not need to take on.*

---

## Section 2 — Which side are you on

**Q2. Which best describes you right now?** *(Required — multiple choice, branches)*
- I rent a room or flat as a tenant → **go to Section 3**
- I rent out property to tenants → **go to Section 5**
- Both — I rent somewhere and also let property out → **go to Section 3**
- Neither → **end the form** with: "Thank you — this survey is for current tenants and landlords."

> *Analysis note: the branch. Report tenant and landlord results separately
> throughout; pooling them produces averages that describe nobody. "Both"
> respondents complete the tenant branch and should be flagged in the data.*

## Section 6 — A shared record (everyone)

> *Introduction shown to respondents:*
>
> The last few questions ask about rental apps in general. There are several on the
> market and this survey is not about any particular one.

**Q15. Have you ever used an app or online service to manage renting — paying rent,
tracking payments, raising maintenance requests?** *(Required — multiple choice)*
- Yes, currently
- Yes, in the past
- No, never
- I'm not sure

**Q16. Suppose a rental app showed both you and the other party the same record of
rent, deposits and payments. Two different ways such an app could handle the money
are described below. Which would you prefer?** *(Required — multiple choice)*

- **The app collects the rent and passes it on.** The app can confirm a payment
  automatically, so nobody has to vouch for it. The money sits with the app's
  company for a short period before reaching the landlord.
- **The money goes directly between tenant and landlord.** The rent never leaves
  your own bank or UPI. Because the app never sees the money, one party has to
  confirm a payment before the record updates.
- No preference
- I don't understand the difference

> *Analysis note: THE question. It carries the project's central design claim, and
> it is written so that each option states one genuine advantage and one genuine
> cost. Do not edit it to be shorter — the costs are what make the answer
> meaningful. "I don't understand the difference" is deliberately offered: without
> it, confused respondents pick at random and their noise is indistinguishable
> from preference. Report its size honestly; if it exceeds about 15%, the question
> was too abstract for this population and the result should be reported with that
> caveat rather than quietly dropped.*

**Q17. What would make you reluctant to use a rental app?**
*(Optional — checkboxes, "select all that apply")*
- I don't want my financial details on an app
- I don't trust the company behind it
- The other party wouldn't use it
- A monthly or per-transaction fee
- Nothing would — I'd use one
- Other: ______

**Q18. Attention check — please select "Rarely" for this question.**
*(Required — multiple choice)*
- Always
- Often
- Sometimes
- Rarely
- Never

> *Analysis note: straightforward but necessary. Responses failing this are
> excluded before analysis, and the number excluded is reported in the methodology
> section. A survey with no attention check cannot distinguish a considered answer
> from a rushed one, and a reviewer who knows that will ask.*

**Q19. Which ONE of these would be most useful to you in such an app?**
*(Required — multiple choice, shuffled)*
- Seeing a clear record of what I've paid and what's owed
- Automatic rent reminders
- Raising and tracking maintenance requests
- Storing the rental agreement and documents in one place
- Paying or receiving rent through the app
- None of these would be useful

> *Analysis note: six options, not ten. A "pick one" question with ten options
> splits the sample too thinly to say anything with confidence at n = 300, and a
> long list invites arbitrary picking. If the result comes back near-uniform
> across the options, that is evidence the question failed, not a finding — real
> preference data has a winner.*

## Analysis plan — settle this before collecting, not after

Deciding what counts as support after seeing the data is how researchers fool
themselves. Fix these now.

| # | Proposition | Tested by | Counts as support |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Record-keeping in independent tenancies is predominantly manual | Q5b (landlords), Q9 (tenants) | A majority of landlords select only non-software options |
| P2 | Tenants commonly hold no landlord-acknowledged proof of payment | Q9 | "Nothing" or "I don't know" is the modal answer, or exceeds 40% |
| P3 | Absent records coincide with disputes | Q7 × Q10 cross-tab | Dispute rate materially higher among those without a copy of an agreement |
| P4 | Users prefer funds to move directly rather than through a platform | Q16 | Direct option leads, **and** the lead is outside the margin of error |

**Before any of that:**

1. Report the response count, the completion rate, and the number excluded by the
   attention check.
2. Report how the sample was recruited and state plainly that it is a convenience
   sample. It is not representative of all Indian renters and should not be
   described as though it were.
3. Check the timestamp distribution. Responses should arrive spread over days,
   clustered around when each recruitment message went out. A block of responses
   arriving within minutes of each other needs explaining before anything is
   computed from it.
4. Check for straight-lining — respondents selecting the same position throughout.
5. **Report differences with their uncertainty.** At n = 300, a gap of under about
   8 percentage points between two options is not a finding. "34% vs 32%" is a
   tie, and writing it as a majority is the single most common way a student
   survey gets taken apart in a viva.
